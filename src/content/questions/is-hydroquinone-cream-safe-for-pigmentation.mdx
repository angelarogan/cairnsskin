## What's actually in these creams?

Many of the stronger pigmentation creams sold at Australian pharmacies contain hydroquinone, an active that works by reducing melanin production in the cells responsible for making pigment. One widely sold pharmacy product describes itself as <cite index="4-2">containing a powerful active ingredient Hydroquinone (2%), for fighting pigmentation at the source</cite>, and is classed as a pharmacy medicine rather than an ordinary cosmetic, which is why it typically sits behind the pharmacy counter rather than on an open shelf.

## What it can and can't do

Hydroquinone genuinely has research behind it for fading certain types of surface pigmentation, such as sun spots and some post-inflammatory marks. But it isn't suited to every kind of pigment. Marketing on these products often promises results in a set timeframe, with one pharmacy listing claiming <cite index="4-4">you should see effective results in 6 weeks</cite>, though individual response varies a lot depending on how deep the pigment sits, your skin type and how consistently sunscreen is worn alongside it. Deeper, dermal pigmentation or melasma often responds poorly to a cream alone and can need a different approach entirely.

## Safety limits worth knowing

Hydroquinone isn't meant to be used indefinitely. The same product instructs users to <cite index="7-4">only use Superfade Face Treatment Cream for 3 months at a time, then give your skin a break</cite>, reflecting a real safety concern: prolonged, unsupervised use of hydroquinone has been linked to skin irritation and, in rare cases, a paradoxical darkening called ochronosis. It's also worth noting product listings themselves advise checking in with a health professional, stating you should consult a Health Care Professional or dermatologist if you have concerns about severe skin pigmentation.

## What to ask before buying anything

Rather than choosing a cream based on packaging or price, it's worth asking what concentration of active is actually in it, whether it's suited to your specific pigment type (sun-related, hormonal, or post-inflammatory all behave differently), and how long it's safe to use before a break is needed. A proper skin consultation can identify whether what you're seeing is genuinely a good candidate for a topical, or whether it's the kind of pigmentation that a cream alone is unlikely to shift, which matters most if you haven't had that assessed before reaching for something off a shelf.

## The bottom line

A pharmacy hydroquinone cream isn't a scam, but it's also not a universal fix. It has a defined use case, a defined safe usage period, and defined limits on the type of pigmentation it can help. Understanding those limits before buying saves money and frustration, and for anything persistent or widespread, a professional opinion on what's actually causing the pigment is worth more than the product itself.
