## Marks and scars are actually two different things

If a breakout has healed but left something behind, the first question worth answering is whether you're looking at a mark or a scar, because they behave very differently and respond to different approaches. Acne marks, sometimes called post-inflammatory discolouration, are flat patches of red, pink, brown or purple that sit on skin that is otherwise smooth in texture. Acne scarring is different: it involves an actual change to the structure of the skin, showing up as indents, pitting or, less commonly, raised tissue.

A simple way to tell them apart is to run a finger over the area with your eyes closed. If the skin feels completely smooth and the difference is only visible, you're most likely dealing with a mark. If you can feel a dip, a dent or firmness, that's textural change, which points to scarring.

## Why marks and scars need different plans

Acne marks are caused by inflammation triggering extra melanin production (appearing brown) or leaving dilated capillaries near the surface (appearing red or pink). These are the same mechanisms behind general pigmentation, and they typically fade on their own over a matter of months, though this can take longer in deeper skin tones or with ongoing sun exposure and repeated breakouts.

Acne scarring happens when inflammation damages the collagen that supports the skin, leaving behind a permanent structural change once the acne itself has healed. This is why scarring is often grouped into recognised patterns such as narrow "icepick" indents, broader "boxcar" depressions with defined edges, or shallower "rolling" scars with softer borders. These don't resolve by themselves and generally need a treatment that works on collagen remodelling rather than pigment.

## What actually helps each concern

For marks, the main levers are protecting skin from further UV exposure, calming ongoing inflammation, and using actives that support pigment turnover, but a shop-bought cream alone has real limits: strength, formulation and how consistently it's used all affect whether an over-the-counter product does much at all. For true textural scarring, in-clinic approaches such as skin needling work by creating controlled micro-injury to encourage new collagen, usually needing a course of sessions rather than a single visit, with gradual improvement over months rather than an instant fix.

Because marks and scarring often overlap on the same face, and because treating one doesn't fix the other, a professional assessment is genuinely useful here, particularly for anyone who has only tried pharmacy products so far and isn't sure which of the two they're actually dealing with.

## When to get it checked

If active acne is still present, that's usually the first thing to bring under control, since ongoing breakouts keep generating new marks and risk further scarring. Once breakouts have settled, a clinician can look at what's texture, what's pigment, and how deep any scarring runs before recommending a realistic course of action.
