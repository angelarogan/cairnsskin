## Acne scars versus acne marks: not the same thing

The word "scar" gets used loosely, but there's an important difference between an actual acne scar and a leftover mark. A true acne scar is a permanent change in the skin's structure, an indent, pit or tethered patch where inflammation damaged collagen during healing. A mark, by contrast, is flat discolouration, either pink/red (post-inflammatory erythema) or brown (post-inflammatory pigmentation), left behind after a pimple settles. Marks typically fade on their own over weeks to months. Structural scarring does not fade on its own, because the physical loss or distortion of tissue doesn't reverse itself the way surface colour does.

## Why textural scars don't just disappear

Atrophic scars, the most common type from acne, form when the skin produces too little collagen to fully rebuild the area after a breakout. Depending on the shape and depth, these are often described as ice pick (narrow and deep), boxcar (wider with defined edges), or rolling (broad, wave-like dips caused by fibrous bands pulling the skin down from underneath). Each behaves differently under treatment, and most people have more than one type mixed together on the same area of skin, which is part of why acne scarring can feel stubborn to improve.

## What genuinely helps with texture

Treatments that aim to improve textural scarring generally work by encouraging the skin to rebuild collagen or by physically releasing the tethering underneath a scar. Skin needling is one commonly used option for this, and radiofrequency-based treatments that combine controlled micro-injury with heat are another approach used to support firmer, smoother-looking texture over a course of sessions. None of these erase a scar completely or work identically for every scar type; ice pick scars, being deeper and narrower, tend to respond more slowly than shallower rolling or boxcar scars.

## Where shop-bought products fit in, and where they don't

A serum or cream bought over the counter can support skin barrier health and may help fade residual redness or pigmentation left after a breakout, particularly with ingredients like niacinamide or gentle retinoids used consistently. What a jar of anything cannot do is rebuild lost collagen or release fibrous tethering under the skin, that's a structural issue, not a surface one. If you're not sure whether what you're looking at is a true scar or a fading mark, that's worth getting an actual opinion on rather than guessing from a shelf. A proper skin consultation can assess scar type and depth and outline realistic, honest expectations for what improvement actually looks like.
