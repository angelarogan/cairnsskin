Pigmentation forms when skin cells called melanocytes produce more melanin, usually
in response to UV exposure. Cairns sits close to the equator and records some of
the highest UV index readings in Australia for most of the year, not just in
summer, which means the main trigger for pigmentation is present far more
consistently than in temperate cities further south.

## UV exposure is the main driver

UV-B radiation directly stimulates melanocytes to produce more melanin as a
protective response. In a climate where the UV index regularly sits in the
extreme range (11 or above), this process happens more often and more
intensely, which is why sun spots, freckling and patchy discolouration tend to
appear earlier and more visibly in far north Queensland than in cooler parts
of the country.

## Heat, humidity and inflammation

Heat and humidity do not cause pigmentation on their own, but they can make it
worse indirectly. Sweating and higher skin temperature can increase
inflammation, and any inflammation, whether from heat rash, acne or skin
irritation, can trigger post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) as the skin
heals. This means existing blemishes or irritation are more likely to leave a
darker mark behind in a hot, humid climate than they would in a cooler one.

## What actually helps

Daily broad-spectrum SPF 50+, reapplied through the day, is the single most
useful habit for slowing new pigmentation. Seeking shade during peak UV hours
and wearing protective clothing or a hat also reduce cumulative exposure.
Gentle, non-irritating skincare helps limit inflammation-related pigmentation
from building on top of sun-related pigmentation.

For pigmentation that has already formed, in-clinic treatments such as
broadband light (BBL) are sometimes used, though outcomes vary between
individuals and treatment is not guaranteed to fully clear established
pigmentation. A consultation is the most reliable way to understand what is
realistic for your specific skin.
