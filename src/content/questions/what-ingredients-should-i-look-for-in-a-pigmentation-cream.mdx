## What actually goes into a pigmentation cream

Most creams marketed for pigmentation rely on a handful of active ingredients that interrupt melanin production somewhere along its pathway. Niacinamide is one of the most common, and it works in part by <cite index="4-4,4-5">reducing the transfer of melanin from melanocytes (pigment-producing cells) to surrounding skin cells called keratinocytes</cite>. It's also generally gentle, and <cite index="4-6,4-7">has anti-inflammatory properties and helps regulate sebum production, and it's usually gentle enough on your skin barrier</cite>.

Vitamin C works differently again, acting as an antioxidant that <cite index="3-9,3-10">plays a number of important roles in skin health, helping protect skin from UV rays, pollution and other environmental stressors</cite>. Arbutin, kojic acid and licorice extract are milder, plant-derived options that also interfere with the enzyme tyrosinase, which drives melanin production.

## Where hydroquinone fits in

Hydroquinone is the ingredient most dermatologists still reach for when over-the-counter options aren't cutting it, largely because of how it works: through <cite index="11-3">reversible inhibition of tyrosinase, an enzyme involved in converting L-3,4-diphenylalanine to the skin pigment melanin, and selective damage to melanocytes and melanosomes</cite>. It's applied in a structured way, typically <cite index="11-4,11-5">topically to the hyperpigmented skin only, twice daily for around three months, after which many people maintain results using it twice a week, and if there's no benefit after three months it should be stopped</cite>. Because it's a stronger active with a real risk profile if used incorrectly, it's not something to self-prescribe indefinitely, and its use is generally intended to be guided and monitored.

## Why the label rarely tells the whole story

A cream can list the right ingredient and still underperform if the concentration is too low, the formulation doesn't allow it to penetrate effectively, or it's being layered with other actives that cancel it out or cause irritation. Slow, mild ingredients can also mask a pigmentation pattern that actually needs a different approach, such as melasma, which behaves differently to sun-related pigmentation and can worsen with the wrong treatment.

## What matters more than the product

Regardless of which cream is used, <cite index="2-13,2-14">many brightening ingredients, including vitamin C, retinoids, and hydroquinone, can increase sun sensitivity, so daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is essential</cite>, especially in a climate like Cairns. If you've been trying shop-bought creams without clear improvement, or you're not sure whether what you're seeing is sun damage, melasma, or something else, a professional skin consultation can identify the actual cause and whether a topical routine, an in-clinic treatment, or a combination is realistic for your skin.
