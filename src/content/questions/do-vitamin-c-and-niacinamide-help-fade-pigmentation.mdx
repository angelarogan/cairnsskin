Vitamin C and niacinamide are two of the most commonly recommended
ingredients for pigmentation, and both have reasonable, though not
identical, evidence behind them. Neither works as a quick fix, and both
need consistent, ongoing use to make a visible difference.

## What vitamin C does

Vitamin C is an antioxidant that can help limit some of the UV-driven
processes that lead to new pigmentation, and it is commonly included in
brightening serums for this reason. It tends to be more effective at
supporting overall skin tone evenness than at fading an established, deep
dark spot on its own.

## What niacinamide does

Niacinamide works differently, by interfering with the transfer of
melanin to the skin's surface layers, which can reduce how visible
existing pigmentation appears over time. It is generally well tolerated
and is often used alongside other actives rather than as a stand-alone
treatment.

## Why humidity changes how these work

In Cairns' heat and humidity, sweat and higher skin temperature can affect
how well topical products absorb and how long they stay on the skin before
being washed away, which is part of why consistency and product choice,
such as lighter, non-greasy formulations, matter more here than in cooler
climates.

## What they cannot do

Neither ingredient replaces daily SPF, which remains the single most
effective habit for preventing new pigmentation and stopping treated
pigmentation from returning. They are also unlikely to meaningfully fade
well-established, deeper pigmentation or melasma on their own, since these
generally need a longer timeframe, more targeted actives, or in-clinic
treatment to see significant change.

## Getting a realistic expectation

Anyone using vitamin C or niacinamide for pigmentation should expect a
gradual improvement over months, not weeks, and should not assume a lack
of overnight results means the products are not working. Where progress
plateaus, discussing in-clinic options at a consultation can clarify what
else is realistic.
