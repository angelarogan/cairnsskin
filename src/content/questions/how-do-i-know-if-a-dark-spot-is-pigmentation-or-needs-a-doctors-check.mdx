## Pigmentation versus something that needs a medical look

Most everyday pigmentation is flat, brown, roughly symmetrical and appears on sun-exposed areas like the cheeks, nose, forehead, hands or chest. It tends to build up gradually over years and stays fairly stable in shape, though it can darken with sun exposure. That's very different to a spot that is genuinely new, changing shape, growing, unevenly coloured, itchy, bleeding or has an irregular, blurred edge. Living in Far North Queensland means most of us carry a fair amount of cumulative sun exposure, so it's genuinely common to have both harmless pigmentation and, less commonly, something that needs a doctor's eye on the same face.

## A simple way to think about it

A useful general guide used by doctors is to look for asymmetry, an irregular border, more than one colour within the one spot, a diameter larger than about a pencil eraser, or any change over time in size, shape, colour or texture. None of these on their own mean something is cancerous, and plenty of harmless moles and pigmented patches will tick one box without being a concern. But if a spot stands out from everything else on your skin, the safest move is always to have it looked at by a GP or dermatologist rather than guessing from a mirror.

## Why this matters before any cosmetic treatment

This distinction matters practically because cosmetic pigmentation treatments such as light-based devices, peels or take-home actives are designed to fade sun-related pigment, not to diagnose or treat a suspicious lesion. A cosmetic clinic focused on pigmentation and tone isn't the same as a medical practice set up to diagnose and manage skin cancer, and the two serve genuinely different purposes. If a spot hasn't been properly assessed and turns out to be something other than simple pigmentation, treating it cosmetically could delay a diagnosis that matters. This is exactly why a proper skin check with a GP, skin cancer doctor or dermatologist is the sensible first step for anything you're unsure about, before pigmentation treatment is even discussed.

## Once you've been given the all-clear

Once a spot or patch has been confirmed as ordinary sun-related pigmentation, there's a lot more flexibility in how it's approached, from daily SPF and targeted actives through to in-clinic options aimed at fading tone unevenness. The right choice depends on how deep the pigment sits, your skin type and how it's likely to respond, which is exactly the sort of thing worth discussing at a proper consultation rather than guessing at home. If you've been living with a patch of pigmentation for a while and haven't had it properly looked at, that's a good reason to get it checked, both for peace of mind and to understand what's actually driving it.
