## What does "acne medication" actually mean?

Acne medication is a broad umbrella term covering everything from shop-bought gels and washes through to prescription creams, oral antibiotics, hormonal treatments and isotretinoin. There is no single product that works for every type of acne, because acne itself has several different drivers, including excess oil, clogged pores, bacteria and inflammation, and sometimes hormonal fluctuation. The right medication depends on which of these is the main problem for your skin, which is exactly the kind of thing a proper skin assessment is designed to work out rather than guess at from a pharmacy shelf.

## Shop-bought options and their real limits

Over-the-counter acne products generally rely on a small handful of actives: benzoyl peroxide, salicylic acid and azelaic acid are the most common. Benzoyl peroxide works by reducing acne-causing bacteria and helping clear pores, and research has found that lower strength formulations tend to work about as well as higher strength ones, with fewer side effects. Salicylic acid assists with exfoliation and helps keep pores clear, while azelaic acid can reduce inflammation and bacteria and is often better tolerated by sensitive skin. These ingredients can genuinely help mild, surface-level breakouts, but they have real limits. They cannot address deep cystic acne, hormonal patterns, or acne that keeps recurring despite consistent use, and picking the wrong concentration or formulation for your skin type can leave you dealing with irritation on top of breakouts rather than clearer skin.

## Where prescription medication comes in

When shop-bought products have not made a meaningful difference after several weeks of consistent use, prescription options are the next step, and this is something only a doctor or dermatologist can assess and provide. Topical prescription retinoids and topical antibiotics are commonly used for mild to moderate acne, often in combination. For moderate to severe acne, oral antibiotics or a hormonal approach such as the combined contraceptive pill or spironolactone may be considered, since hormonal factors can be a significant driver in adult acne, particularly in women. For severe, cystic or scarring acne that has not responded to other treatments, isotretinoin is the most extensively studied option, though it is prescribed with caution and requires medical supervision due to its side effect profile.

## Why the right assessment matters more than the product name

Because acne types overlap and can change over time, the same product can work brilliantly for one person and do very little for another. Rather than cycling through pharmacy products by trial and error, a professional consultation can look at what type of acne you actually have, how your skin is reacting to what you have already tried, and whether the concern is being driven by something a topical alone cannot fix. This is especially useful for anyone who has been self-managing with shop-bought products because they have not yet had that assessment, since understanding the actual mechanism behind an active ingredient matters far more than the name on the packaging.
