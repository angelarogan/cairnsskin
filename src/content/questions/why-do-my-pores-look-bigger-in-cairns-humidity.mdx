Pore size itself is largely structural and genetic, but how visible pores
look can change noticeably day-to-day, and heat and humidity are two of
the biggest everyday influences on that.

## Why heat and humidity make pores more noticeable

Higher temperatures increase oil (sebum) production, and that extra oil
sits in and around the pore opening, making it catch light differently
and look more obvious. Heat and humidity also cause mild, temporary
swelling in the surrounding skin, which can make the pore opening itself
look wider than it does in cooler, drier conditions. Neither effect
permanently changes pore size, but together they explain why pores can
look noticeably larger on a hot, humid day in Cairns than they might
elsewhere.

## The build-up cycle

Extra oil combines with sweat, dead skin cells and everyday grime, and in
a consistently warm, humid climate this build-up happens more often and
clears more slowly than it would in a cooler climate. Over time this
build-up can stretch the pore lining further, which is part of why
persistently oily, congested skin tends to show more visibly enlarged
pores than dry or normal skin types.

## What actually helps

Regular gentle exfoliation and oil-balancing, non-comedogenic skincare
help limit the build-up that makes pores look worse, without over-drying
skin in a way that can trigger even more oil production. Daily sunscreen
protects the collagen around the pore opening, which helps it keep some
natural elasticity over time. In-clinic options such as microdermabrasion
or a hydrating facial are sometimes used to refine overall texture and
reduce the appearance of build-up around pores, though no treatment
changes the underlying structural size of a pore.

## Setting a realistic expectation

Because pore size is structural, the realistic goal is minimising their
appearance rather than eliminating them, and what works best depends on
individual skin type and oil production. A consultation is the most
reliable way to find out what's likely to help your specific skin.
